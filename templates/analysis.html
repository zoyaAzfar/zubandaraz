Newspaper corpus analysis

Who acts, and who is acted upon?

How Urdu news writes about women and men, measured four ways: grammar, adjectives, word embeddings and topics.

Larger bodies of work can carry linguistic bias, and newspapers are an excellent example. Are women written about as people who "do" things, or as people things happen "to"? Are certain words used typically around women that aren't used for men? This phenomenon has been studied previously in English, and emerging studies have done so for Arabic, Turkish and Hindi. Even though Urdu is spoken by over 250 million people worldwide, it has received much less attention.

In this analysis, we ask a simple question: how do the words used around men and women in Urdu news differ? We answered this question by measuring four things.

Grammatical agencyIs a gendered term more often the subject or object? ModifiersWhich adjectives are found more commonly around female versus male terms? Semantic associationIn a Word2Vec space, which concepts are female or male terms nearer to? TopicsWhat do articles about men and women talk about?

Data and methods

Our articles come from a public dataset of Urdu news, containing {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.total) }} articles across four categories: Business & Economics, Science & Technology, Entertainment, and Sports. Each article was flagged using a list of female and male terms. {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.female) }} articles contained a female term and {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.male) }} a male term; {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.both) }} contained both, leaving {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.f_only) }} female-only and {{ '{:,}'.format(corpus.m_only) }} male-only articles. So only about 20% of the original corpus was used for this analysis.

Agency via dependency parsing

200 articles from the female-term set and 200 from the male-term set were sampled and parsed with Stanza's Urdu pipeline (no parsing failures). For each gendered term, we recorded whether it was used as a grammatical subject or object. Only documents with one countable role were assigned an "agency rate" — subjects ÷ (subjects + objects/passives) — which left about {{ ag.f_docs }} female and {{ ag.m_docs }} male articles. The group difference in mean document-level rate was tested with a 10,000-permutation test.

Distinctive modifiers

Adjectives attached to female and male terms were counted (102 female and 53 male modifier tokens; 64 vs. 33 distinct words) and compared with a log-odds score. A positive score means a word is more distinctive of female terms; a negative score, of male terms. At this sample size, these statistics are best read descriptively.

Word2Vec semantic association

A skip-gram Word2Vec model was trained on the full corpus with a min_count of 20. Female and male attribute sets (47 and 45 terms) were reduced by the frequency cutoff to 37 and 34 in-vocabulary terms. We defined five categories to test — Leadership/Power, Domestic/Family, Crime/Legal, Victimhood/Vulnerability, and Professional/Career — for 94 concept tests in total. For each test we calculated the mean cosine similarity of the concept to each attribute set, then the difference Δ = female − male. Significance came from a randomization test that reshuffles which terms carry a male or female label, followed by a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across all 94 tests at α = 0.05. Randomization p-values shift by a few thousandths between runs.

Topic modelling

BERTopic, with UMAP dimensionality reduction, was run separately on 500 female-term and 500 male-term articles. Outlier documents were reassigned so every article belonged to a topic. Each topic is a cluster of frequently co-occurring keywords, which we labelled in plain English and then grouped into six broader themes. Since labelling is up to interpretation, the individual topics are listed in the appendix below.

Agency

Across all parsed mentions, female terms were grammatical subjects {{ ag.f_subj }} times and objects or passives {{ ag.f_obj }} times ({{ '%.1f'|format(f_rate) }}% agentive). For male terms, this was {{ ag.m_subj }} and {{ ag.m_obj }} ({{ '%.1f'|format(m_rate) }}%).

At the document level, the mean agency rate was {{ '%.2f'|format(ag.f_mean) }} for female articles and {{ '%.2f'|format(ag.m_mean) }} for male articles, a difference of −0.173 (permutation p = {{ '%.3f'|format(ag.p) }}). Nearly half of the female documents — 30 of 62 — contained only object or passive mentions, while over half of the male documents — 26 of 49 — contained only subject mentions.

A bootstrap on the same 111 documents gives a 95% interval for the difference of roughly −0.34 to −0.005, which only just excludes zero. If each mention is treated as independent, the comparison is not significant (Fisher's exact p ≈ 0.22). Mentions inside an article aren't independent, so the document-level test is the better one, but this remains a suggestive result given the small sample.

Modifiers

The biggest problem we ran into here was that gender is built into Urdu grammar. Adjectives that scored as strongly feminine or masculine, such as بڑی and بڑا ("big," feminine and masculine forms), often just reflected grammatical agreement with whoever was being described, not any real bias. Since there's no list of gendered Urdu terms we could use to exclude, we were forced to rely on manually created stopword lists, which were not perfect — so the hatched bars below are exactly this grammatical-agreement artifact, not a distinctive-usage finding.

By log-odds score, female terms leaned towards words such as سوتیلی ("step-"), نوجوان ("young"), and خوبصورت ("beautiful"), as opposed to the more ordinary male terms, which included مبتلا ("afflicted"), شفیق ("kind"), and انجہانی ("the late," i.e. deceased). However, these correlations did not reach FDR significance, so this should be read as descriptive insight rather than confirmed.

Word2Vec

Across all 94 tests, we found that our average difference was −0.011, and 63 of 94 concepts sit closer to the male attribute set. Leadership/Power (17 of 20) and Crime/Legal (18 of 19) are almost exclusively male, but individual effect sizes are small, so most words do not survive correction. Domestic/Family is the only category where more concepts lean female than male (12 of 19).

Primarily, six associations survive correction (q < 0.05). Terms such as چیئرمین ("chairman"), کاروبار ("business"), دفتر ("office") and روزگار ("employment") — all professional, career-oriented terms — were male, with 0.095–0.221 similarity. Conversely, the two female terms were گھریلو ("domestic") and مظلوم ("oppressed/wronged"), which relate to family and victimhood themes respectively, with 0.272–0.382 similarity.

Topic modelling

Our topic modelling results found that film and celebrity coverage is the largest theme in both samples (34% female, 32% male), followed by sport (21% vs. 23%). The differences are present in the smaller themes. Articles mentioning women include more crime stories (11% vs. 6%), driven by harassment and assault cases, as well as fashion and culture. Articles mentioning men spread into a broader bucket of general Pakistan news, relating to technology, science, economics and COVID-19.

Discussion

Although each of our results is modest alone, they broadly point to a trend of women being under-represented in public-sphere roles and often being framed as marginalized or as victims. Women are often mentioned more in object or passive roles (agency test), sit nearer household and victimhood vocabulary (Word2Vec), and appear more often in stories about harassment and courts (topic modelling).

However, the male attribute set may simply be the baseline here. The two attribute sets differ in size, term mix and frequency, and embedding similarity tends to track frequency. The average Δ of −0.011 could be a baseline shift rather than "everything is more male." A useful next step is to centre Δ on the mean and re-test.

Limitations

  • The corpus is dominated by entertainment and sport, so results describe how gender appears in that coverage, not particularly in politics or economics reporting.
  • Our frequency cutoff dropped 10 female and 11 male terms, mostly kinship words such as "mother" and "father." Since both attribute sets lose much of their family-related vocabulary, this may soften any domestic-versus-public contrast.
  • Our agency significance is at exactly p = 0.050, which means it's quite fragile. Urdu's free word order, ergative marking and light-verb constructions also make subject/object labelling harder for a parser than in English.
  • The theme assignments for topics are manually grouped, and outlier reassignment can sometimes force the model to assign articles to a topic when it may not be that clean.
  • We ran one Word2Vec training run. Since embedding similarities shift between random seeds — and even randomization p-values shift by a few thousandths between runs — repeated runs would give a better sense of stability.
  • Due to hardware limitations, we were only able to parse about 400 of the gendered articles.

Future work

  • Lemmatize modifiers so grammatical gender agreement would not show up in our bias results.
  • Retrain Word2Vec over several seeds to report the spread and change in the similarity.
  • Increase the number of articles, given more time and better equipment, to help improve the statistical significance of some results.
  • Compare these findings to English-medium Pakistani papers, another interesting analysis.
