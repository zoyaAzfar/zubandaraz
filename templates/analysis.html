Our Analyses

Urdu Newspaper Analysis

We looked at 111,861 Urdu newspaper articles through four different lenses to see whether women and men are written about differently.

At a glance

  • Word associations. Six of 94 concept words lean reliably toward one gender’s terms. Chairman, business, office and livelihood sit nearer to men’s terms; domestic and victim words sit nearer to women’s.
  • Who does the acting. Women’s terms are grammatical subjects a little less often than men’s (47% against 57%). The gap sits at the edge of significance and changes with the test used.
  • Adjectives. The two adjectives that survived correction, بڑا and چھوٹا, are masculine forms whose feminine twins sit on the other side. That is grammar, not description.
  • Topics. Articles with female terms lean toward entertainment (57% against 40%) and have almost no economy, technology or science coverage (3% against 21%).

What is new here

As far as we could find, studies of gender in Urdu newspapers have been critical-discourse and collocation studies on hundreds to low thousands of articles; one recent comparison of Urdu and English papers used 1,440. This corpus is about 78 times larger, and the approach differs in four ways.

  • Four lenses on one corpus. Syntax (who is the grammatical subject), embeddings (which words live near which), topics (what the stories are about) and word choice (which adjectives attach). When the lenses disagree, we say so.
  • Corrections for multiple testing throughout. We ran 94 word-association tests and 63 adjective tests. Before correction, 25 of the 94 look significant; about 5 would by chance.
  • Grammatical gender treated as a hazard. Urdu adjectives change form with the gender of the noun, so a naive “which adjectives describe women” test partly measures agreement. Work on other languages has found that grammatical gender can swamp topical gender signals in embeddings, and we show what that looks like in Urdu.
  • More than one unit of analysis. For agency we report pooled mentions and per-article rates, because the answer changes between them.

The main embedding pattern, work and leadership words with men and family words with women, is consistent with what has been reported for Arabic-language news, so it may be regional rather than a quirk of this corpus.

Data and methods

The corpus is 111,861 Urdu news articles. We matched articles against lists of female and male terms, then ran four analyses. Random seed 42, 10,000 permutations, and a 5% false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg) throughout.

Agency
Stanza dependency parses of 200 articles with female terms and 200 with male terms. Each gendered word was marked as a grammatical subject or as an object or passive. Agency rate is subjects divided by subjects plus objects and passives, per article, compared with a permutation test over articles.
Word associations
A word2vec model trained on the whole corpus (minimum count 20; 37 female and 34 male terms in its vocabulary). For 93 concept words in five themes, we compared similarity to the female terms against similarity to the male terms. Significance comes from 10,000 random relabelings of the terms. عدالت appears in two themes, so there are 94 tests.
Topics
BERTopic on 500 articles with female terms and 500 with male terms, fitted separately. Topic labels are ours, from top keywords and representative articles, and we grouped them into five broad themes by hand.
Adjectives
Adjectives attached to gendered words, 63 of them appearing at least twice. Log-odds scores and Fisher’s exact tests, with false discovery rate correction.

Results

The corpus

Of the 111,861 articles, 14,289 contain a female term and 15,530 a male term, and 7,060 contain both. That is 22,759 gendered articles, about a fifth of the corpus; the rest contain none of our terms. Everything below describes those articles.

Word associations: office and business lean male, home and victimhood lean female

For each concept word we asked whether it sits nearer the female terms or the male terms. Six of 94 comparisons survive correction (Table 1). Four are about work and authority and lean male; two, one about the household and one about victimhood, lean female. Twenty-five of the 94 clear p < .05 before correction, against about five expected by chance, so this is more than noise.

There is a catch. Male terms sit slightly closer to nearly everything (dashed line, average −0.011), so “leans male” has to be read against that baseline. Relative to it, domestic and family words are the clearest female-leaning theme (about +0.02), while leadership, and crime and law, lean male by about 0.014. The counts on each row are descriptive: words within a theme are related, so they are not independent tests.

Who does the acting: a borderline gap

In the 400 parsed articles, women’s terms were grammatical subjects 47% of the time (48 of 103 classified mentions) and men’s 57% (39 of 69). Only about a quarter of gendered mentions could be classified as subject or object, so this rests on 172 mentions.

Most articles contribute a single classified mention, so article-level rates are nearly all 0 or 1 (Figure 4). A long article counts the same as a short one.

Three defensible tests of the same gap give three different verdicts:

  • Permutation test on article-level rates, 10,000 shuffles: mean difference −0.17, p = 0.050.
  • Bootstrap 95% interval for that difference: −0.34 to −0.005.A check we added for this report.
  • Fisher’s exact test on pooled mentions (48 of 103 against 39 of 69): p = 0.22.A check we added for this report.

Our reading: a small difference in the expected direction, sensitive to how it is measured. One possible driver is subject matter. Abuse and harassment coverage makes up 39 of the 500 articles in the female topic sample, and in those stories women are often the ones acted upon.

Topics: a portrait, not a test

Both samples are dominated by entertainment, and Bollywood is everywhere, but the rest of the mix differs. Articles with female terms are 57% entertainment and lifestyle, 21% sport and 15% harassment, court cases and rights campaigns. Articles with male terms are 40% entertainment and 23% cricket, and about a fifth cover economy, technology, science and national affairs, themes with almost no counterpart on the female side. The share of women’s sport is likely inflated by naming: women’s teams are called women’s teams, while men’s teams usually are not.

Adjectives: a lesson in grammar

Of 63 adjectives that appeared at least twice, two survived correction, both on the male side: بڑا (1 female against 12 male) and چھوٹا (1 against 11). But Urdu adjectives take the ending alif (ا) before masculine nouns and ye (ی) before feminine ones, and the feminine twins بڑی and چھوٹی are among the top female-side adjectives. The test is picking up agreement. Add that these adjectives often mean elder and younger in family contexts, and the pattern is as much about kinship words as about description.

The other candidates never came close. On the female side, young (نوجوان) and beautiful (خوبصورت) have q-values of 0.40 and 0.72, and none of the female-side adjectives went below 0.30. The nearest male-side candidate after the two grammatical ones, مبتلا (afflicted), has q = 0.07. We treat this section as a methods note, not a finding.

Limits and next steps

  • Small parsed sample. 400 articles, and only about a quarter of gendered mentions received a subject or object label. A larger parse would tighten the agency estimates.
  • Female-term article is not the same as an article about a woman. Some articles appear to enter a sample through family words; one topic in the female sample covers a male cricketer’s family.
  • Missing letters. Many words in the source text have lost the letter آ (اج for آج, سٹریلیا for آسٹریلیا). This blurs some word pairs and hurts parsing. The text needs repair and the analyses need re-running.
  • Adjective p-values. The p-values need to be checked against the counts shown; on some rows the counts and p-values do not line up.
  • Separate topic models. Fitting one joint model with gender as a covariate would allow direct comparison.
  • Association is not intent. Embeddings reflect which words appear together in what gets reported. They show patterns in coverage, not what editors meant.

What we would and would not say

  • Confidently: in this corpus, words about business, office, employment and chairmanship live nearer to men’s terms, and household and victim words nearer to women’s.
  • Cautiously: women’s terms may be slightly less often grammatical subjects.
  • Not yet: anything about which adjectives describe women, until grammatical gender is handled.
