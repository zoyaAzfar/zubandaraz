Newspaper Corpus Analysis

Large bodies of work can also carry linguistic bias, and newspapers are an excellent example. Are women written about as people who "do" things, or as people to whom things happen? Are certain words used around women that rarely appear around men? This kind of linguistic bias has been studied in English, and more recently in Arabic, Turkish and Hindi. Urdu, spoken by over 250 million people worldwide, has received far less attention.

In this analysis we ask a simple question: how do the words used around men and women in Urdu news differ? We measured four things: grammatical agency (is a gendered term more often the subject or the object?), modifiers (which adjectives cluster around female versus male terms?), semantic association (in a Word2Vec space, which concepts sit closer to female or male terms?), and topics (what do articles about men and women actually talk about?).

Data & methods

The articles come from a public dataset of 111,861 Urdu news articles spanning Business & Economics, Science & Technology, Entertainment, and Sports. Each article was flagged against a list of female and male terms: 14,289 articles contained a female term and 15,530 a male term; 7,060 contained both, leaving 7,229 female-only and 8,470 male-only articles. That's about 20% of the original corpus.

200 articles from the female-term set and 200 from the male-term set were parsed with Stanza's Urdu pipeline (no parsing failures) for grammatical agency. For each gendered mention we recorded whether it was a subject or an object. Documents with a countable role were assigned a document-level "agency rate" — subjects ÷ (subjects + objects/passives) — which left 62 usable female articles and 49 usable male articles. The group difference in mean document-level rate was tested with a 10,000-permutation test.

Adjectives attached to female and male terms were counted (102 female and 53 male modifier tokens; 64 vs. 33 distinct words) and compared with a log-odds score, where a positive score is more distinctive of female terms and a negative score of male terms. At this sample size, these statistics are best read descriptively.

A skip-gram Word2Vec model was trained on the full corpus with a minimum word count of 20. Female and male attribute term sets (47 and 45 terms) were reduced by the frequency cutoff to 37 and 34 in-vocabulary terms. Five concept categories were tested against those attribute sets — Leadership/Power, Domestic/Family, Crime/Legal, Victimhood/Vulnerability, and Professional/Career — for 94 concept tests in total. For each test we computed mean cosine similarity of the concept to each attribute set, then the difference Δ = female − male. Significance came from a randomization test reshuffling which terms carry the male or female label, followed by a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across all 94 tests at α = 0.05.

BERTopic, with UMAP dimensionality reduction, was run separately on 500 female-term and 500 male-term articles. Outlier documents were reassigned so every article belonged to a topic. Each topic was labelled in plain English by hand, then grouped into broader themes.

Results

Agency

Across all parsed mentions, female terms were grammatical subjects 48 times and objects or passives 55 times (46.6% agentive). Male terms split 39 and 30 (56.6% agentive).

At the document level, mean agency rate was 0.44 for female articles and 0.61 for male articles — a difference of −0.173 (permutation p = 0.050). A bootstrap over the same 111 documents gives a 95% interval of roughly −0.34 to −0.005 for that difference, which only just excludes zero; treating each mention as independent instead of each document, the same comparison is not significant (Fisher's exact p ≈ 0.22). Since mentions inside one article aren't independent, the document-level test is the better one, but this remains a suggestive rather than a settled result given the small sample.

Modifiers

Urdu grammar itself is gendered, which made this the hardest test to run cleanly: adjectives that scored as strongly feminine or masculine, such as بڑی and بڑا ("big," feminine and masculine forms), often just reflect grammatical agreement with whoever is being described rather than any real bias. With no ready-made list of gendered Urdu terms to exclude, we relied on manually built stopword lists, which are imperfect — so the two most frequent words below are exactly this grammatical-agreement artifact, not a distinctive-usage finding.

By log-odds score — a better measure of distinctiveness than raw frequency — female terms leaned toward words such as سوتیلی ("step-"), نوجوان ("young"), and خوبصورت ("beautiful"), against more ordinary male-leaning terms like مبتلا ("afflicted"), شفیق ("kind"), and انجہانی ("the late," i.e. deceased). None of these correlations reached FDR significance, so read them as descriptive rather than confirmed.

Word2Vec

Across all 94 tests, the average Δ was −0.011, and 63 of 94 concepts sat closer to the male attribute set. Leadership/Power (17 of 20) and Crime/Legal (18 of 19) were almost exclusively male-leaning, though individual effect sizes are small and most words don't survive correction. Domestic/Family was the only category where more concepts leaned female than male (12 of 19).

Six associations survived FDR correction (q < 0.05). Male-leaning: چیئرمین ("chairman," Δ −0.083), کاروبار ("business," Δ −0.062), روزگار ("employment," Δ −0.061), and دفتر ("office," Δ −0.055) — all professional/career vocabulary. Female-leaning: گھریلو ("domestic," Δ +0.061) and مظلوم ("oppressed/wronged," Δ +0.068) — domestic and victimhood vocabulary. Similarities across these six terms ranged 0.095–0.221 on the male-leaning side and 0.272–0.382 on the female-leaning side.

Topics

Film and celebrity coverage was the largest theme in both samples (34% female, 32% male), followed by sport (21% vs. 23%). The gap between the two samples shows up in the smaller themes: articles mentioning women included more crime stories (11% vs. 6%), driven largely by harassment and assault cases, as well as more fashion and culture coverage. Articles mentioning men spread further into a broad bucket of general Pakistan news: technology, science, economics, and COVID-19.

Discussion

Each result on its own is modest, but together they point toward a pattern: women under-represented in public-sphere roles, and more often framed as marginalized or as victims. Women are mentioned more often in object or passive roles (agency), sit nearer household and victimhood vocabulary (Word2Vec), and appear more often in stories about harassment and courts (topic modelling).

That said, male vocabulary may simply be acting as the baseline in this embedding space. The two attribute sets differ in size, term mix and frequency, and embedding similarity tends to track frequency — so the average Δ of −0.011 could reflect a baseline shift rather than "everything is more male." A useful next step is to centre Δ on the mean and re-test.

Limitations

The corpus is dominated by entertainment and sport, so these results describe how gender appears in that coverage, not necessarily in politics or economics reporting. The frequency cutoff dropped 10 female and 11 male terms, mostly kinship words like "mother" and "father" — since both attribute sets lose much of their family vocabulary, this may soften any domestic-versus-public contrast. The agency result lands at exactly p = 0.050, which is fragile, and Urdu's free word order, ergative marking, and light-verb constructions make subject/object labelling harder for a parser than in English. Topic themes were grouped by hand, and forcing every outlier document into a topic can sometimes assign articles to a topic that isn't a clean fit. We also ran a single Word2Vec training run — similarity values shift between random seeds, so repeated runs would give a better sense of stability — and hardware limitations meant we could only parse about 400 of the gendered articles for the agency test.

Future work

Lemmatizing modifiers would stop grammatical gender agreement from showing up as a false bias signal. Retraining Word2Vec over several seeds and reporting the spread in similarity, rather than a single run, would give a better sense of how stable these associations are. Parsing and sampling more articles would improve statistical power on the borderline results. And comparing these findings against English-medium Pakistani papers would be an interesting extension.
